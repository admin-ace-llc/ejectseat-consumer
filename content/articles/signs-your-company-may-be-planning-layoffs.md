No single sign tells you a layoff is coming, but public records can show it earlier than rumors do. The most reliable signals are formal ones: an SEC filing that discloses a restructuring plan, or a WARN notice that gives at least 60 days of warning. Softer signs, such as a hiring freeze or sudden reorganizations, are only hints. This guide separates what documents can prove from what is guesswork.

## What are the most reliable signs of coming layoffs?

Two kinds of public record are worth checking, because they are required rather than leaked.

- **SEC filings by public companies.** A company that commits to a restructuring or workforce reduction may disclose it on a Form 8-K. The filing for exit and disposal costs is Item 2.05. The official form is on [sec.gov](https://www.sec.gov/files/form8-k.pdf). Our explainer, [what is SEC 8-K Item 2.05](/insights/what-is-sec-8-k-item-2-05), covers how to read one.
- **WARN notices.** Under the federal WARN Act, the Department of Labor says covered employers with 100 or more employees must give at least 60 calendar days of advance written notice of a plant closing or a mass layoff affecting 50 or more employees at a single site. The rules are on the [Department of Labor's plant closings and layoffs page](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/termination/plantclosings). Some states have their own rules.

Browse the [live layoff feed](/layoffs) and the [WARN page](/warn), or [check a specific company](/check.html).

## Does a WARN notice mean my job is gone?

Not always. A WARN notice covers a defined site and group of employees, and it does not say who is affected. If you work at a different site, you may not be covered by it. It does mean the employer has told the government that job losses are planned, so read the notice carefully. Our guide on [how to read a WARN notice](/insights/how-to-read-a-warn-notice) explains the fields.

The Department of Labor lists exceptions to the 60-day rule, including unforeseeable business circumstances, faltering companies and natural disasters. So a layoff can happen with less notice, and the absence of a notice is not proof that none is coming.

## What can a company filing tell me, and what can't it?

A restructuring filing can tell you that a plan exists, and often gives a date and an estimate of costs. It usually does not tell you which teams, locations or people are affected. A filing can also be about closing a facility or exiting a business line, not only cutting staff.

Read for these items:

- **Which type of action it is:** workforce reduction, facility closure or broader restructuring.
- **The timing:** when the plan was approved and when it is expected to finish.
- **The cost estimate:** companies often give a range, and the final number can differ.
- **What is missing:** if the filing gives no headcount, do not assume one.

## Which workplace changes are only hints?

These are common in companies that later cut jobs, but they are also common in companies that do not. Treat them as reasons to prepare, not proof.

- **A hiring freeze or paused backfills.**
- **Budget or travel cuts announced company-wide.**
- **A reorganization that merges or removes management layers.**
- **A new executive team or a change in strategy.**
- **Poor financial results, or a sharp drop in a product line.**
- **Sudden closed-door meetings, or access changes to shared tools.** These can have many causes.

Do not act on rumors about named individuals, and do not share unverified claims about colleagues.

## What should I do if I see warning signs?

Preparation is low cost and does not require you to assume the worst.

- **Save personal records:** your offer letter, pay stubs, performance reviews and a list of your accomplishments. Only keep documents that are yours, not confidential company files.
- **Learn your benefits timeline:** how health coverage and retirement accounts work if you leave. See [health insurance after a layoff](/insights/health-insurance-after-a-layoff-cobra-vs-marketplace).
- **Know the steps for unemployment:** rules vary by state. Start with [unemployment insurance after a layoff](/insights/unemployment-insurance-after-a-layoff).
- **Read the severance basics now:** see [what to ask for before you sign a severance agreement](/insights/what-to-ask-for-before-you-sign-a-severance-agreement).
- **Keep a plan for the first two days:** [what to do in the first 48 hours after a layoff](/insights/what-to-do-in-the-first-48-hours-after-a-layoff).

Rules on notice, benefits and severance vary by state and employer. For advice on your situation, contact your state labor department or an employment lawyer.

## What to watch

Check new SEC filings and WARN notices for your employer on a regular schedule, and compare any claim you hear internally against those documents. A filing or notice is evidence. A rumor is not.
