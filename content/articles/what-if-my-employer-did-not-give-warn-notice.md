If your employer was required to give 60 days' notice of a mass layoff or plant closing and did not, the federal WARN Act generally lets affected employees claim **back pay and benefits for each day of the violation, up to 60 days**. Whether the law applied to you depends on the size of the employer, the number of jobs lost at your site, and whether an exception covers the employer. Rules vary by state and employer, so treat this as a starting point and confirm the details with the sources linked below.

## What does the WARN Act require?

The Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act is a federal law enforced mainly through private lawsuits. In broad terms:

- It covers employers with **100 or more employees**, not counting part-time workers.
- It requires **60 calendar days' written notice** before a covered plant closing or mass layoff.
- Notice goes to affected employees (or their union representative), the state dislocated worker unit, and the chief elected official of the local government.
- A mass layoff generally means job losses at a single site that reach set thresholds, such as 50 or more employees making up at least a third of that site's workforce, or 500 or more employees.

For plain-language summaries from the government, see the Department of Labor's [plant closings and layoffs page](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/termination/plantclosings) and its [Worker's Guide to Advance Notice of Closings and Layoffs](https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/ETA/layoff/pdfs/WorkerWARN2003.pdf).

## What can an employee recover if notice was not given?

Under the statute, an employer that violates the notice requirement is generally liable to each affected employee for:

- Back pay for each day of the violation, up to 60 days
- The cost of benefits the employee would have received, such as medical expenses that would have been covered, for the same period

The amount can be reduced if the employer paid wages during the notice period or made voluntary, unconditional payments. Employers may also face a civil penalty owed to the local government. Because the law is enforced through court actions, workers who think they were shorted typically consult an employment attorney or a legal aid organization.

## What are the exceptions?

The law allows shorter notice in limited situations. The main ones are:

- **Faltering company**: applies only to plant closings, when the employer was actively seeking financing that would have avoided the shutdown and reasonably believed that giving notice would have blocked it.
- **Unforeseeable business circumstances**: a sudden, unexpected event outside the employer's control.
- **Natural disaster**: such as a flood or earthquake.

Even when an exception applies, the employer is still expected to give as much notice as practicable and to include a short statement of why the notice is late. The full rules are in the [Department of Labor's employer guide](https://www.dol.gov/sites/dolgov/files/ETA/layoff/pdfs/_EmployerWARN2003.pdf) and the federal regulations at [20 CFR Part 639](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/chapter-V/part-639).

## Why might WARN not apply to you?

- The employer has fewer than 100 employees.
- The job losses at your site fell below the thresholds.
- You were a part-time employee, or worked remotely without a clear tie to a covered site.
- You were fired for cause or left voluntarily.

Many states have their own notice laws with lower thresholds or longer notice periods, so a layoff that falls outside federal WARN may still be covered by state law. Check with your state labor department.

## What should you do next?

- **Keep your documents**: termination letter, pay stubs, offer letter, benefits summary and any notice you did receive.
- **Note the dates**: when you were told, your last day, and your last day of pay and coverage.
- **Look for the notice**: state agencies publish many WARN filings. See EjectSeat's [WARN page](/warn) and our guide on [how to read a WARN notice](/insights/how-to-read-a-warn-notice).
- **Read before you sign**: a severance agreement may ask you to release claims. See [what to ask for before you sign a severance agreement](/insights/what-to-ask-for-before-you-sign-a-severance-agreement).
- **File for unemployment**: do this promptly regardless of any WARN claim. See [unemployment insurance after a layoff](/insights/unemployment-insurance-after-a-layoff).

This article is general information, not legal advice.
